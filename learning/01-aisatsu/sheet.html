Out the Door and Back

いってきます／ただいま

The song is one day. It opens with a door closing and ends with the same door opening — and everything in between is a greeting that somebody else has to answer. That's the whole reason あいさつ works as a song: these phrases already come in pairs.

𝄆
01

Three structures, one song

あいさつ looks like a vocabulary list and isn't. There are three different shapes hiding in it, and each one gets its own section of the song.

The door — pairs

いってきます／いってらっしゃい

True call-and-response. One person says it, the other must answer. This is the hook, and it's the whole product.

The clock — a cycle

あさ・ひる・よる・ねるまえ

Four greetings walked through one day, in fixed order. The day is the memory palace.

The register — a suffix

おはよう ⟶ おはようございます

Same word, two politeness levels, chosen by who you're talking to. One suffix, three greetings.

Style — three to race

Generate all three before deciding. They're not variations on a look; they're three different answers to the question of how much silence sits between a call and its response, which is the only thing that actually matters here.

A · Shōwa kayōkyoku — the warm house Used for v1

This is what generated. Tempo, diction and mora-timing all landed — only the voice-swap failed, and that was the lyrics' fault rather than the style's. Use A2 below with the v2 lyrics.

B · Doo-wop — the texture workaround Promoted

Now the second choice rather than the third. If the duet keeps failing, stop fighting timbre: answering group vocals are an arrangement texture, not a second soloist, so Suno handles them reliably — and the learner joins the group rather than watching two other people talk. The cost is that it sounds nothing like Japan.

Style prompt B
1950s doo-wop, 88 BPM, lead vocal with answering group vocals,
walking bass, finger snaps, soft brushed snare, tenor sax pad,
sweet and simple, dry lead vocal, minimal reverb,
extremely clear enunciation,
wide space between call and response, no ad-libs

C · Ondo — the festival version

Call-and-response is structural, the meter is natively 7–5, and it comes with dance steps — which is the gesture layer arriving free rather than being designed.

Style prompt C
Japanese ondo festival song, 90 BPM,
call and response between a lead singer and a group,
taiko drum, shinobue flute, shamisen, hand claps,
celebratory but unhurried, dry vocals, very clear enunciation,
big gaps between call and response, no ad-libs

05

What take one proved

Generated 27 August 2026 with style prompt A. Three of the four risks named in advance came back clean; one broke, and it broke for a reason worth knowing.

  • TempoConfirmed92 BPM is right. "Super easy to follow along." Don't let a more musical take at a faster tempo win — that trade is the whole failure mode of this genre.
  • いってきますConfirmedThe っ survived. A real beat of silence — it-te, not ite. This was the main risk to the entire premise: a song that swallows the sokuon teaches wrong mora-timing, which is worse than teaching nothing. The reserve fix (いっ てきます with a space) wasn't needed.
  • こんにちワConfirmedThe particle fix works and doesn't sound robotic. Mixed hiragana/katakana mid-word didn't degrade the singing. The two-document rule is real, and for a kana-only song it costs exactly three changed lines.
  • A ⟷ BBrokeThe voices never alternated. One singer throughout. Not a mystery and probably not a tier limit — see below.
  • おはようUntestedThe long-vowel risk didn't surface, but wasn't specifically listened for. If the う ever articulates as its own syllable: おはよーございます, in the score only.
  • Intro / OutroUntestedSuno will most likely sing these rather than speak them. That matters more than it looks — the spoken tag is the pitch-accent mitigation. If it sings, record those four seconds separately and edit them on.

Why the duet failed — and why the fix makes the song better

The song asked Suno to change voice every single line. That's finer than it can switch.

Practitioner consensus is that a part change has to sit in a block of roughly two lines. Finer than that and the model either holds one voice — what happened here — or degrades into 朗読劇, a reading play rather than a song. Nothing observed points at the free tier; the granularity explains it completely.

So: double every line. Each voice holds two before handing over. And that isn't a compromise — doubling the line doubles the repetition, which is the method. The learner now hears each phrase twice in one voice before the answer arrives. The constraint improved the song.

If it still fails, stop fighting timbre and change texture instead. Make the response a group answering a lead, doo-wop style. Backing vocals are an arrangement texture rather than a second soloist, so Suno handles them reliably — and a group answering the lead is arguably better pedagogy anyway, because the learner joins the group instead of watching two other people talk. That's style prompt B, and it just went from third choice to second.

Paid Personas would give a further lever — lock a voice per role — but they shouldn't be bought to solve this particular symptom until the doubling and the texture swap have both been tried.

06

One judgment call I'd flag

Verse 2 — the ございます layer — is arguably a different song.

The hook and the clock verse teach a situation. Verse 2 teaches a rule about politeness, which is a grammar lesson wearing the same clothes. It's the strongest single idea in the draft and it may be the thing that makes this song too full: eleven phrases plus a register rule is a lot for two minutes at N5.

I've left it in because it's cheap to cut and expensive to invent twice. If the gap test comes back weak, verse 2 is the first thing out — and it becomes its own episode, where one suffix and three greetings would carry a whole song comfortably.

𝄆
07

Your traditional-chant idea, parked properly

It's a second strand, not a second topic — and it's a good one.

いろは歌 and 北原白秋's 五十音 (「あめんぼ赤いな あいうえお」) are doing something the situational songs can't. They're not teaching phrases; they're teaching the sound system itself. 五十音 in particular was built as an articulation exercise — it's still what voice actors and announcers use to warm up, and it walks every row of the gojūon deliberately. That's the one thing a song can teach better than anything else, and it's the exact gap the pitch-accent problem leaves open.

Both are public domain — いろは is a thousand years old, and Hakushū's 五十音 is on 青空文庫 and Wikisource, which only carry expired copyright. So restyling them is clean.

Why park it anyway: it's a different product with a different test. A situational song succeeds if someone can fill the gap; a chant succeeds if someone's mouth moves better afterwards. Those need separate formats and separate proof. Get one situational song working first, then this strand gets its own planning pass — and it deserves one, because "五十音 as a series of style exercises" is genuinely a better idea than another vocabulary song.
