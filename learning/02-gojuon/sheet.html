Amenbo no Uta

五十音 ― 北原白秋

Hakushū's 五十音 walks every consonant row against every vowel, in order, and makes the mouth work for each one. It is still what Japanese voice actors and announcers warm up with. This is the one thing a song teaches better than any other medium — and it is exactly the gap the pitch-accent problem leaves open.

𝄆
01

A different product, judged differently

あいさつ teaches phrases in a situation. This teaches the sound system itself. The success test changes with it: not can you fill the gap, but does the mouth move better afterwards.

Don't try to make it teach vocabulary. It will fail at that and stop doing the thing it's for.

あめんぼ, ささげ, なめくじ, まいまい, らいちょう, いどがえ — none of this is N5 and none of it was ever meant to be. The payload is the mouth, not the words. Gloss the vocabulary on the sheet for curiosity's sake and let it go.

The design problem

五十音 has no hook. Ten stanzas of entirely new material, zero repetition — pure enumeratio, the exact opposite of sticky. Every other song in the series is built on a returning chorus. This one arrives with none.

The answer was already sitting in the poem. The five-kana run is the refrain. アイウエオ / カキクケコ / サシスセソ — the same five-beat figure, the same vowel order, a different consonant every time. Anaphora at the level of pattern rather than text, and also the entire lesson.

Leadthe tongue-twister line
Allthe five kana
Leadthe second twister line
Allthe five kana  — closing the verse

Nothing is cut and nothing is reordered. The kana run keeps Hakushū's original position and additionally repeats to close the verse. That repeat is the only thing added to the poem.

And it's built on the thing that "went wrong" last time.

Suno puts both voices in unison on the last line of a verse regardless of your tags — that's what turned the あいさつ duet into an arrangement instead of a conversation. Here that habit isn't a defect to route around. Unison is where a listener is invited to join in, and this song wants everyone chanting the kana. So the kana run goes last, and the model's own bias does the recruiting.

Split it in half and join the two halves.

Retracted 2026-08-27. That was my advice and it was wrong — you tried it and it came out too different. Two Suno generations share no seed, no voice, no tempo and no arrangement; they're two songs that happen to have consecutive lyrics, not two halves of one. There was never anything to merge.

Generate it in one pass. Nobody has actually confirmed 42 lines fails — I engineered around a problem that was assumed, not observed. If it genuinely is too long, the mechanism is Suno's own Extend → Get Whole Song, which conditions on the audio you already have. Read the sheet while it plays and check for dropped lines.

The only particle conversions needed are three: すを → すオ, ふえを → ふえオ, らいちょうは → らいちょうワ. Note that ヲ in the ワ行 refrain stays as written — there it's a letter being named, not a particle being used.

𝄆
04

How this text was verified — read before generating

Reconstructed and checked against the poem's own constraint. That's real evidence, and it is not proof.

Every automated fetch refused to reproduce the verbatim text, so the check that actually ran was this: each stanza has to alliterate on its own row's consonant. カ行 gives かきのき・くりのき・きつつき・こつこつ・かれけやき — all k. All ten pass.

That check does more than confirm the text — it settles a reading. 蝸牛 can be read かたつむり or まいまい, and only まいまい fits the マ行 stanza. A か-reading would break the row. The poem's structure is its own proofreader, which is a rather beautiful property for a memory text to have.

Still: check it once against a source before spending a generation. Wikisource · with furigana.

Copyright isn't in question. Hakushū died in 1942; Japan's term was then life+50, so it lapsed in 1992, and the 2018 extension to life+70 did not revive expired copyrights. Aozora Bunko and Wikisource both carry it, and both host only public-domain work.

𝄆
05

Two calls that are yours, not mine

1 · Stanzas 9 and 10 teach kana that no longer exist

ヤ行 is ヤ イ ユ エ ヨ — the イ and エ are placeholders; modern ヤ行 has only ya · yu · yo. ワ行 is ワ ヰ ウ ヱ ヲ, where ヰ (wi) and ヱ (we) are obsolete and ヲ survives only as the object particle.

A learner drilling those in 2026 without being told is being taught letters they will never see. I've marked both stanzas on the sheet rather than choosing for you:

  • Keep and label — recommended. The history is genuinely interesting, the poem stays whole, and one line of context turns a trap into the best trivia in the song.
  • Keep silently — dishonest, and it's the kind of thing a learner finds out later and resents.
  • Stop at ラ行 — safe, and it mutilates a complete work to avoid a footnote.

2 · Which style leads

The four below all take the same lyric file. That's what makes this a recurring series slot rather than a single song — one fixed public-domain text, re-dressed indefinitely, which is exactly the idea you raised.

My argument is for hip-hop, and it isn't a joke: 五十音 is a flow exercise.

𝄆
06

Four styles, one lyric

A · Boom-bap hip hop Recommended

The non-obvious one, and I think the right one. Rappers drill articulation with tongue-twisters for the same reason actors do, and a consonant row is alliteration practice by construction. The twister lines want to be rapped; the kana run wants to be the hook. This is the version someone might play for pleasure rather than practice — which is the only way a drill ever gets repeated enough to work.

Style prompt A
boom bap hip hop, 88 BPM, clear-voiced rapper on the verses
with a full group chanting the hook in unison,
dusty upright bass, crisp snare, minimal piano loop,
dry forward vocal, no reverb on the lead, no autotune,
extremely clear consonants, hard rhythmic diction,
space between the verse and the chant, no ad-libs

B · Matsuri ondo

How a chant like this actually gets drilled: one voice calls, everybody answers. Hand claps land on the kana, the meter is natively 7–5, and it arrives with dance steps attached.

Style prompt B
Japanese matsuri ondo, 92 BPM,
one lead singer calling and a large group answering in unison,
taiko drum, shinobue flute, shamisen, hand claps,
festive and driving but unhurried, dry vocals,
extremely clear enunciation, big gap before each group answer, no ad-libs

C · Gospel choir

Leader and congregation. The unison answer stops being a drill and becomes the emotional payoff — which is a real argument for it, because nobody resents singing this version.

Style prompt C
gospel call and response, 84 BPM,
solo lead vocal answered by a full choir in unison,
Hammond organ, tambourine, hand claps, upright piano,
joyful and warm, dry lead vocal, very clear enunciation,
big choir answers, no ad-libs

D · Bossa nova

Warm, adult, and closer to home than the others. But it carries a real cost worth naming: bossa's syncopation pulls against the mora grid, and mora-timing is half of what this song exists to teach. Generate it for pleasure, not for the drill.

Style prompt D
bossa nova, 96 BPM, soft male vocal with a group answering,
nylon string guitar, brushed drums, light upright bass, subtle flute,
relaxed and warm, dry vocal, very clear enunciation, no ad-libs
