Irasutoya Visual Encyclopedia Song series — planning N5 · Japanese-only · standalone

Sing It Twice

おぼえる うた ― 計画

A Japanese-learning song series built from the Irasutoya catalog, using the memory techniques that predate writing. The premise is narrow on purpose: a beginner who already reads kana should be able to sing along on the first listen, and use a whole sentence pattern by the third.

𝄆
01

Why this works better in Japanese than in English

Before picking topics, it's worth being clear about why a song is a good container for this particular language — because it isn't equally true of every language.

Japanese conjugation already rhymes. English mnemonic songs have to force rhyme onto the grammar; in Japanese the grammar is the rhyme scheme.

Japanese is a suffixing language. Meaning changes at the end of the word — which is exactly where a song lands its rhyme. So a verse that hammers one ending isn't a gimmick wrapped around a grammar point. It's the grammar point, sung.

Five grammar points, one melodic figure, one syllable of difference each time. A learner who can hum that shape can swap たべ for のみ, いき, かい and generate two dozen sentences they were never taught. That transfer is the whole product.

The second gift: mora-timing

Japanese is mora-timed, not stress-timed. Every kana gets one beat — including the small っ (a beat of silence) and the long vowel ー (a beat of held sound). Beginners almost universally get this wrong, because their ear imports English stress timing.

You cannot lecture someone into mora-timing. You can put them on a grid and let the beat do it. A song is a mora metronome — arguably the single thing songs teach better than any classroom method.

And Japanese has a native meter waiting for you: 七五調 (shichigochō), the seven-five pattern underneath folk song, school song, enka, and most classical verse. Write to 7–5 and the lines land as Japanese instead of as English wearing kana.

One kana, one cell, one beat. The grid is 原稿用紙 — the squared manuscript paper Japanese children learn to write on. It is already a counting device; a song just adds the tempo.

You are joining a tradition, not inventing one

Japan has taught by chant for a thousand years. いろは歌 is a poem that uses every kana exactly once — a pangram built as a memory song. 九九 is the multiplication table as a rhythmic chant every Japanese child still learns aloud. 語呂合わせ turns numbers into phrases so they can be remembered as language. This is a live pedagogical form there, not an import.

02

The toolkit: seven devices, each with a job

These are the rhetorical figures that oral cultures used to carry long texts without writing. Each one below is paired with the specific thing it does to Japanese — a device that doesn't earn its keep grammatically shouldn't be in the song.

AnaphoraSame head, changing tail

Repeat the opening, vary the ending. In most languages this is a stylistic flourish. In Japanese it is the shape of the conjugation table, which is why it's the single highest-value device here.

みずを のみます ／ みずを のみません ／ みずを のみました

Use for: any verb-form song, politeness levels, negation.

AntiphonyCall and response · 掛け合い

Two voices, one asks and one answers. The oldest teaching form there is — work songs, liturgy, military cadence, Bon-odori. Japanese hands you this for free, because the question particle か makes every question audibly a question.

Crucially, it teaches both sides of a real exchange. A learner who only ever practises the customer's line freezes when they're asked something.

Use for: shop, restaurant, station, directions — anything with two roles.

The slot swapRepetition with one variable

The most important idea on this page. Repetition without variation teaches a sentence. Repetition with exactly one thing swapped teaches a pattern — and a pattern is generative.

おはしは いりますか ／ スプーンは いりますか ／ レシートは いりますか

Hold the frame rock steady, change one noun per line. By the third line the learner has stopped hearing a phrase and started hearing a blank.

Rule: never swap two things at once. Two variables and the frame dissolves.

LociMemory palace · Simonides

Attach items to positions in a remembered space, then walk the space to recall them in order. The song becomes a route: verse 1 the entrance, verse 2 the kitchen, verse 3 the bath.

This is where Irasutoya stops being decoration and becomes the method — every location already has its illustration set, so the palace can actually be seen.

Use for: morning routine, the house, the station, asking directions.

EnumeratioCatalogue & cumulative song

Each verse adds an item and re-recites everything before it. Brutal, effective, and the reason cumulative songs survive centuries. Expensive in runtime — spend it on lists that genuinely must be memorised whole.

Use for: counters (助数詞), days of the week, numbers, months — the closed sets where there's no pattern to infer, only a list to hold.

Mimetic hook擬音語・擬態語

Japanese has an enormous vocabulary of sound-and-manner words, most of them reduplicated — which means they are already hooks before you write any melody.

どきどき ／ ぺこぺこ ／ ぐるぐる ／ きらきら ／ わくわく

These are the stickiest possible Japanese chorus material, and textbooks systematically underteach them even though native speech is saturated with them.

Use for: the chorus of almost anything. Feelings, weather, body.

The gapLeave the line unfinished

An unfinished phrase is stickier than a complete one — the brain will not leave it alone. Sing the frame, drop the last beat, let the learner fall into it.

コーヒーを ＿＿＿＿

The third repeat of a chorus should have a hole in it. This is also your only honest test of whether the song is teaching: if they can't fill the gap, they were listening, not learning.

04

One song architecture, reused every time

A series needs a shape a listener recognises by episode three, so their attention goes to the Japanese instead of to the song. Roughly 2:00–2:30 at 90–100 BPM. The numbering here is real — this is a fixed running order, not a list.

00

Cold open 2 bars

The sound of the situation, then the one phrase the episode is named for. No music yet.

01

Hook 8 bars

Four lines, sung twice. This is the entire product — everything else is scaffolding around it. Under seven words per line.

02

Verse — the script 16 bars

The exchange as it actually happens, slow enough to shadow. Two voices if the situation has two roles.

03

Hook 8 bars

Identical. Resist the urge to develop it.

04

Verse — the slot swap 16 bars

Same frame, one noun changed per line. The generalisation step, and the difference between a jingle and a grammar.

05

Bridge — role reversal 8 bars

The learner now sings the other person's part. Half the reason people freeze in Japan is that they only ever rehearsed their own line.

06

Hook with the gap 8 bars

Last repeat, last beat missing. The listener finishes it or they don't — and that's your test.

07

Tag 2 bars

The single phrase they should still have tomorrow. Spoken, not sung.

𝄆
05

Style track

The half of the Suno prompt that isn't words. Genre is a real pedagogical choice here, not decoration — it decides tempo, how many voices you get, and how much silence sits between phrases for the learner to breathe into.

Genre candidates

Shōwa kayōkyoku歌謡曲

Warm, mid-tempo, unhurried, natural room for a male–female duet. The most forgiving bed for call-and-response, and it sounds like Japan without sounding like a textbook CD.

Ondo / minyō音頭

Bon-odori festival music. Call-and-response is structural, the meter is natively 7–5, and it comes with dance steps — which is free gesture-memory.

Dōyō童謡

Children's song. Simplest melodies, clearest diction, highest stick. The risk is condescension — fine for a kid, potentially insulting to an adult learner.

City popシティ・ポップ

Adult, cool, currently beloved outside Japan. Buys you an audience who'd never click a "learn Japanese" video. Watch the tempo — it wants to run fast.

Doo-wop / soul revue

Call-and-response is the genre's native grammar, and backing vocals repeating the hook behind the lead is exactly the pedagogy you want, for free.

Reggae / dub

Enormous space between phrases, slow by nature, and the echo repeats your line for you. Structurally the best fit on this list; the cultural distance from the subject is the only cost.

Non-negotiable production constraints

  • Tempo85–100 BPM. If a learner can't sing along on first listen, the song has failed regardless of how good it is. Suno defaults faster than this — say the number.
  • VoicesTwo, clearly different. One is the world, one is the learner. Keeping the roles audibly distinct is what makes the bridge's role-reversal land.
  • VocalDry and forward. Explicitly ask for minimal reverb and clear enunciation. Reverb is the enemy of a consonant.
  • ArrangementSparse. Every instrument competing with the vocal is a word lost. Bass, drums, one harmony instrument.
  • SilenceLeave room after each phrase. A learner needs a beat to echo into. Ask for space between lines.

Anatomy of a style prompt

Shōwa kayōkyoku, 96 BPM, male and female call-and-response duet,
upright bass, brushed drums, electric piano, warm and slightly comic,
dry forward vocals, minimal reverb, very clear enunciation,
sparse arrangement, space between phrases, no ad-libs

06

Lyrics track — and the trap in it

This is the part that will bite you three songs in if it isn't settled now, so it's worth stating plainly before any lyrics get written.

Every song needs two lyric documents, and they must never be the same file.

Suno is an English-trained model reading Japanese text more or less literally. To make it pronounce Japanese correctly you have to misspell the Japanese — write the particle は as ワ, push ambiguous kanji into katakana, spell numbers out phonetically. Those are exactly the spellings a learner must never see. So: one Suno score (phonetic, for the machine) and one learner sheet (correct orthography, for the human). Same song, two encodings.

The pronunciation rules that matter

ProblemWrite insteadWhy
は as a particle は ワ Sung literally as ha otherwise. Same for へ→エ and を→オ. Protect the real は inside words like はし.
Multi-reading kanji 空 ソラ Suno guesses the reading from context and guesses wrong. Push the ambiguous ones to katakana; leave unambiguous kanji alone.
Digits ２人 フタリ Numerals get read in English. Japanese number readings also shift by counter, so spell the intended one.
Latin letters AI エーアイ Read as English words. アイ alone would be sung as ai — "love".
Long vowels & small tsu コーヒー ／ がっこう Write them explicitly and let them own a beat. This is where a song either teaches mora-timing or teaches it wrong.
Long lines 10–20 characters Short lines with a break per phrase; commas as breath marks. Section tags [Verse] [Chorus] steer the structure.

Don't convert wholesale to katakana — it stabilises pronunciation but flattens the singing into something robotic. The working compromise is hybrid: fix the particles and the ambiguous kanji, leave hiragana verb endings alone.

𝄆
07

What songs can't do

Worth being straight about, because two of these are real and one of them is a genuine cost you'd be choosing to accept.

Pitch accent Real cost

Japanese has lexical pitch accent — はし means bridge or chopsticks depending on the contour. A melody overwrites that contour completely. Songs are excellent for vocabulary, phrase-shape and mora-timing, and they will actively teach wrong pitch accent while doing it. There's no way around this; the only honest responses are to accept it, or to put the tag line and the cold open in speech, not song, so at least the takeaway phrase carries a correct contour. Recommend the second.

Productive grammar Manageable

Songs are strong at storing chunks and weak at teaching someone to build a novel sentence under pressure. The slot-swap verse is the mitigation — it's the only part of the architecture that pushes toward production rather than recall. Don't cut it to save runtime.

The fossilised phrase Manageable

A phrase learned inside a melody can stay welded to that melody, and come out sing-song in real speech. The spoken tag at the end of each song is partly there to break the weld — hear it once without music before you leave.

Depth By design

Twelve songs is maybe 150 words and eight patterns. That's a real N5 foundation and it is not a course. Positioning this as "the thing you play in the car until the phrases are automatic" is honest; positioning it as a curriculum isn't.

08

What to build first

One recommendation, and the argument for keeping the slate closed until it's tested.

Build one song all the way through before writing a second.

There are twelve good topics above and every one of them will feel like it should be written today. The thing that decides whether this series is worth twelve songs is whether one song makes a phrase stick — and you can find that out in an afternoon, not a month.

  1. Write コンビニ, complete. Learner sheet, Suno score, style prompt. It's the funniest, the most useful, and the best showcase of antiphony plus slot swap — and it has 157 illustrations sitting behind it.
  2. Generate three takes at different tempos — 88, 96, 104 — and keep the slowest one you can stand. The instinct will be to keep the most musical take; resist it.
  3. Run the gap test on a real person. Play it twice, wait a day, then sing ふくろは ＿＿＿ and see what comes back. If they fill it, the format works. If they don't, the format is wrong and no amount of additional topics fixes that.
  4. Only then decide whether あいさつ is episode 0 (a 90-second format proof) or whether コンビニ opens the series outright.
