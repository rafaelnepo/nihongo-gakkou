Three Strands

うた ― せいさく の みちすじ

The project just became three products with three audiences, and only one of them is published. The pipeline is a cost. Deciding which strand pays it is the most consequential thing on this page — everything else is machinery.

𝄆
01

Rule zero: where each strand exits

Eleven stages carry a learning episode from a blank page to YouTube. A song for family passes through four of them and then it is finished.

The risk to the family songs isn't quality. It's that they never get sent.

If a family song has to clear a kanji audit, a timing pass and a thumbnail template before it counts as done, it will sit in a folder. Write it, generate it, listen once, send it. That strand's whole value is that it arrives while the feeling is still true.

𝄆
02

The three strands

Different audience, different privacy, different success test. Mixing them would wreck all three — which is why they are now separate folders rather than a naming convention.

Learning learning/

Japanese learners · N5 · strangers

Situational songs plus restyled traditional chants. Sing along on the first listen, use the pattern by the third. Japanese-only lyrics — the illustration carries the meaning, never a translation.

Test: can they fill the gap a day later?

Public · full pipeline

Personal personal/

your wife · your son

An English journal entry becoming a short Japanese song — a thought shared in her language. Something new for your boy.

Test: did it land?

Private · never published

Yuba yuba/

The farm · the community

Moods and feelings. Not a whimsical fit: Yuba is an eighty-year-old Japanese-Brazilian community with its own theatre, choir and arts tradition. Music is already what the place does — this strand joins something rather than importing it.

Scope undecided: for the community, or about it.

Case by case

Three things the personal strand needs that the others don't

Adapt, don't translate. A journal entry is English prose; a song is compressed and rhythmic. Translating the entry faithfully will produce awkward Japanese and a stiff song. Carry the one true feeling in six lines and it lands far harder than twenty accurate ones.

Have the Japanese read by a native speaker before sending. The stakes are simply different. A learning song with an odd phrase is a curiosity; a love letter with one is something else.

Single voice, short, and no phonetic tricks that leak into meaning. The は→ワ misspellings exist to fix a machine. In a drill a mispronounced syllable is a footnote; in a song meant to carry feeling it costs more.

𝄆
03

The two-document rule just became three

Your video spec — no romaji, no furigana, kanji held to a grade — created a third lyric encoding that didn't exist before, and it's the strictest of them.

FileForRules
learner.txtStudyCorrect orthography, romaji, gloss. Kanji is fine — it's a page, you can stare at it.
suno.txtThe machineDeliberately misspelled. は→ワ, ambiguous kanji→katakana, digits spelled out.
screen.txtThe videoCorrect orthography, no romaji, no furigana, no gloss, kanji held to the song's grade. Read in real time with zero support.

screen.txt is the one that decides whether the video teaches anything. Everything a learner cannot decode in the second it is on screen is wasted — which makes "limit the kanji" the single highest-leverage constraint in the whole video spec.

So it's now a machine check, not a vibe

Your grade idea maps onto a real system: the MEXT 学年別漢字配当表, which assigns every one of the 1,026 kyōiku kanji to a school year. That means a song's reading level is a fact a script can verify. I built it — songs/tools/kanji_grade.py:

$ python3 tools/kanji_grade.py learning/01-aisatsu/screen.txt --grade 1

8 distinct kanji in screen.txt

  grade 1   ( 7)  日月人先生学校
  UNKNOWN   ( 1)  調
            ^ not in any loaded grade list. Do not assume.

FAILS grade 1: 調
Fix by rewriting those words in kana, or raise the song's grade.

Fixed kanji panel for the video (copy as-is):
  日  月  人  先  生  学  校

It also prints the video's fixed kanji panel, so that list is generated rather than hand-kept — which is the difference between a feature and a chore you'll abandon by episode four.

One deliberate choice in the data: grade 1 ships complete and verified at 80 kanji; grades 2–6 are empty stubs. A wrong kanji in a linter is a silent bug that teaches the wrong thing, so it reports UNKNOWN rather than guessing. Fill them from the MEXT table when a song needs a higher grade.

𝄆
04

The video, and what actually builds it

Suno doesn't do video. Your spec — words highlighting as sung, waveform, timecodes, elements pulsing to the beat, a fixed kanji panel — is a template problem, not an editing problem.

Timing Do it by hand first

The word-level timing file — not the video — is the pipeline's real artifact. It decouples when each word is sung from how the video looks, so you can rebuild the video a hundred times without re-timing a thing.

Hand-time song one. Forty lines, twenty minutes in any audio editor. Automating this before the format has proven itself is exactly the mistake I made with the split-and-merge advice — engineering around a problem nobody had measured.

When you do automate: this is forced alignment, not transcription. You already know the text, which is a much easier problem than guessing it. WhisperX gives word-level timestamps; Montreal Forced Aligner is reported more accurate for precisely this case.

Video Remotion

React, programmatic, renders MP4, and word-level caption libraries already exist for it. The right tool because the series needs the same video a dozen times with different content — which is what a template engine does well and what a timeline editor does badly.

It also puts the video in the same language as the rest of your work, so the kanji panel, the waveform and the beat-pulse are components rather than keyframes you re-draw per episode.

The visuals — and this is the good one

They should come from the Irasutoya catalogue. Thirty-six thousand illustrations, already indexed, already mapped to every topic on the slate, already queryable by tools/pipeline/index/q.py for the exact word being sung.

Right now the song series only borrows from the encyclopedia — it takes the topic list and the counts. Sourcing the video frames and the thumbnails from the catalogue is the step where it stops borrowing and starts being made of it. Same for stage 9: thumbnail = illustration + the song's kanji + the series mark, generated from a template, never hand-made.

𝄆
05

Style stays open, on purpose

Koto and traditional, kids' songs, classic crossed with modern — that exploration is the right call and it shouldn't be closed early. But two decisions are quietly in tension and it's worth naming before it bites:

"Nail down one style so it sounds like a set" and "try a bunch of things" pull opposite ways.

The resolution in house-style.md: one voice, many arrangements. Hold the Persona constant and let the genre move. That's how a single artist's record can range widely and stay unmistakably theirs — and it's what a Persona is actually built to do, since vocal character travels across genres better than arrangement does. Explore freely; just explore under one voice.

Which is why the seed track comes first. It's a throwaway — one short single-voice generation whose only job is to be a clean identity source, because duets make poor Persona seeds and both existing songs are duets. Nothing else can be locked until that exists.

And the two cheapest identity devices are still unbuilt: the sting and the sign-off. Three seconds each, generated once as standalone assets and edited onto every episode. They cost almost nothing, they do more for "this is a series" than any voice-locking feature, and unlike a Persona they survive every Suno model change.

The paid tier is now justified by a measured need rather than a guess: mid-song pronunciation repair is paid-only, and without it one bad syllable costs a full regeneration — which then arrives with a fresh set of random defects.

𝄆
06

Next four things, in this order

Each one is cheap and de-risks the next.

  1. Seed track → Persona. One single-voice throwaway. Until this exists, every generation is a different band.
  2. Sting + sign-off into assets/. Three seconds each. The door from あいさつ is already sitting there.
  3. Write screen.txt for あいさつ and lint it at grade 1. This is the first time the video spec touches a real song, and it will change the lyric.
  4. Hand-time that one song. Twenty minutes. Only then build anything in Remotion.

The Remotion template is deliberately last. It's the most expensive thing here and the easiest to build wrong — and it can't be designed properly until a real timing file exists to build it against.

And the one still outstanding from two sessions ago: the gap test on あいさつ. Play it twice, wait a day, say ただいま, see whether おかえりなさい comes back. Everything on this page assumes the format works, and that assumption is still untested.
